Applied Field Project

Building Operational Continuity in a Member-Based Organization

An eight-week organization development field project translating diagnostic findings and employee knowledge into repeatable operational processes.

Overview

This project involved a small, member-based nonprofit with limited staff and significant dependence on knowledge held by individual employees. Earlier diagnostic work identified concerns involving member retention, onboarding clarity, communication channels, event participation, and member-value visibility. The field project focused on a practical risk underneath those concerns: essential work lacked consistent documentation and could become difficult to sustain if a key employee became unavailable or left.

Organizational Context

The organization operated through staff knowledge, recurring administrative routines, ChamberMaster, email, spreadsheets, and board participation. Its small size allowed quick adaptation, but also concentrated knowledge in very few people. New-member onboarding and recurring billing depended on timing, judgment, system knowledge, and informal practices that had not been assembled into complete written procedures.

The continuity issue did not involve employee capability. It involved the organization's limited ability to retain and transfer what capable employees knew.

Methods and Frameworks

The engagement used an Action Research model supported by Appreciative Inquiry and process-consultation principles. Inquiry began with prior diagnostic findings and continued through interviews, a focus group, working meetings, process walkthroughs, document review, and iterative validation with staff.

  • Exploratory interviews and stakeholder discussions clarified how work occurred.
  • Process walkthroughs connected system actions with timing, purpose, and exceptions.
  • Existing documents and communications helped verify recurring practices.
  • Draft procedures returned to staff for correction and practical review.

Evidence and Findings

Institutional knowledge created continuity risk

Critical steps existed in employee memory, dispersed notes, and system familiarity. A replacement employee could see available buttons without understanding when to use them, how one action affected another, or when an exception required escalation.

Member intent lacked consistent capture

The organization gathered application information but lacked a dependable method for recording what each member hoped to gain. That gap limited tailored onboarding, interpretation of participation, and discussion of value with members who did not attend events regularly.

Communication and process remained inseparable

Billing notices, onboarding messages, member records, and staff follow-up worked as one operating process. Documenting only a system transaction would miss the communication decisions and handoffs that gave it meaning.

Work Produced

The primary deliverables included detailed billing and invoicing and new-member onboarding standard operating procedures. They addressed routine actions, timing, decision points, exception handling, responsibilities, and the relationships among ChamberMaster, email, and supporting records. Screen references supported system use without allowing screenshots to replace the written process.

The procedures also provided a foundation for future member-intent capture by identifying where that conversation belongs and where the resulting information should travel next.

Recommendations

  • Assign an owner and review date to each procedure.
  • Validate procedures with someone who did not help write them.
  • Add member-goal capture to onboarding and renewal conversations.
  • Interpret engagement through member intent instead of attendance alone.
  • Revise procedures during the planned transition away from ChamberMaster.

Professional Reflection

The project reinforced that operational documentation represents an OD intervention when it changes who can understand, perform, evaluate, and improve the work. A useful procedure does not merely instruct a user where to click. It makes the process visible enough for another person to understand its purpose, relationships, risks, and points of judgment.
